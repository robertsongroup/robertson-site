There is a piece of advice that gets handed to every owner who reaches a couple of million in revenue, and it sounds like wisdom. Hire an operations manager. Get yourself out of the day to day. Work on the business instead of in it, move up into sales and strategy, and let someone else run delivery.

I understand why people say it. The logic is clean, it flatters the owner, and it points at a real problem. What I have come to believe is that for most owners it is the wrong first move, and that when it goes wrong it goes wrong in two places at the same time.

## Why the advice is so appealing

Every owner I talk to at this stage is tired in a specific way. Not the tiredness of hard work, which most of them enjoy. The tiredness of being the answer to every question, of being the place decisions go to get made, of not being able to take a week off without the week coming with them.

The operations manager promises to lift that. Somebody competent arrives, absorbs the running of the place, and the owner gets their attention back. It also promises growth, because the owner then takes all that recovered attention and points it at bringing in new business.

That is a genuinely good outcome. It is just not usually what happens next.

## The first failure is where you moved to

Think about how you got here. Almost nobody builds a business to two million dollars because they were an excellent salesperson. They build it because they were good at something. They did quality work, or made a quality product, and people noticed. Customers came through reputation, through referral, through the network they already had, and through being visibly better at the thing than the alternatives.

That is a real and hard-won advantage. It is also not selling, or at least not the kind of selling that gets you from two million to five.

When you move yourself into the sales seat, you are moving into the one function where you have the least experience, at exactly the moment it starts to get difficult. The easy clients are gone. Your personal network has been worked. Referrals still come, though not at the rate you need, and they arrive on their own schedule rather than yours. What is left is the part of sales that requires actual method, and method is the thing you never had to build because you never needed it.

I have watched capable owners spend a year in that seat and come out with very little to show for it, then conclude they are bad at sales. Usually they are not bad at sales. They are untrained at a version of it they have never done, while carrying the whole rest of the business in their head at the same time.

## The second failure is what you left behind

The other half is quieter and it usually surfaces later.

The person you hired to run operations knows how to do the work. That is generally why you hired them, because they were good at the job and they earned the promotion, or because they came from a competitor and understood the trade. Knowing how to do the work is not the same skill as knowing how to scale it, and the second one is much rarer than the first.

Scaling operations means building process where there was judgment, making standards explicit that used to live in your head, working out how quality survives when you are not the one checking it. That is a design job. Most people who are excellent at delivery have never done it, and being handed the title does not teach them.

What happens instead is that your operation keeps running roughly the way it ran, minus you. That is fine at first. Quality holds because the habits hold. Then volume moves, or a difficult customer arrives, or two people leave in the same month, and the gaps show up. The owner is off chasing new business and does not see it early, because the whole point of the arrangement was that they stopped looking.

I have seen this produce the worst possible version of the outcome, which is losing existing customers while failing to win new ones. You end up worse off than when you started, and it is very hard to see coming from the inside because both halves look reasonable in isolation.

## What I would do instead

Unless your own background is genuinely in sales, I think you are better served hiring that capability and keeping yourself close to the work until operations has both the structure and the people to scale properly.

That means hiring a sales rep who can find and work opportunities, manage a pipeline, chase the things that need chasing, and bring you in at the point where you are actually the asset. You are very good in the room with a serious prospect. You know the work, you can answer any question, and people believe you because you are not performing. That is worth a great deal, and it is worth much more when someone else has done the twenty hours of unglamorous work required to get you into that room.

Meanwhile you stay where your judgment is worth the most, which is close enough to delivery to see quality drifting before a customer tells you. You use that time to build the thing operations actually needs, which is not a manager on top of the current arrangement but a set of standards and systems that would survive your absence. Once that exists, promoting or hiring someone to run it is a much smaller bet, because you are handing over a system rather than a folklore.

## The objection worth taking seriously

People will point out that founder-led selling is the most effective form of selling in a small business, and they are right. Nobody sells your work like you do. If you take that to its conclusion, the argument says you should be in sales and the whole point stands.

The part it leaves out is volume. Founder-led selling is the most effective per conversation and the least scalable per week, because there is one of you and you are also the technical authority, the escalation point and the final word on quality. Effectiveness in a single conversation does not help if you can only have four of them a month.

A rep does not replace you in the room. They multiply the number of rooms. They will close business you would never have reached, and on the deals that genuinely need you, they get you there prepared instead of you getting there by accident.

## The real point is sequence

None of this says never hire an operations leader. It says the order matters more than most people admit, and the default order is backwards for the majority of owner-operated businesses at this size.

Stay on what you are good at for longer than the advice tells you to. Build the sales capability alongside you instead of moving into it. Get the operation to a state where it could be handed over, then hand it over. That is a slower story than the one where you promote someone and step up into growth, and it fails a lot less often.
