You made the hire. Maybe it was a sales rep so you could stay close to the work, maybe it was an operations manager so you could move into sales yourself. Either way you now have something you did not have before, which is a person whose job is bringing in business.

Then nothing happens. A quarter goes by. The pipeline looks thin, the deals that do appear are the same kind of deals you were already getting, and the growth you built the plan around has not arrived. You start wondering whether you hired the wrong person.

Usually you did not. You made the hire in the wrong order.

## The reframe

Here is the thing I wish someone had told me earlier. You do not hire a sales rep when you need sales. You hire one when you no longer have enough time to work the leads and opportunities you already have.

That is a completely different trigger, and it changes who you should be hiring.

If leads are not outrunning your capacity to handle them, a salesperson does not fix anything. You have taken an expensive person and pointed them at an empty field. They will spend their week prospecting cold, which is the lowest-yield activity available to them and almost certainly not what you interviewed them for. The numbers will not come, and at some point you will conclude the hire failed.

What you actually needed was marketing.

## The two triggers

There are two situations that say a sales hire is genuinely due. If you are in one of them, hire. If you are in neither, you have a different problem.

**One. You are closing business yourself, and losing some of it because you cannot keep up with the leads coming in the door.** Enquiries arrive faster than you can respond to them properly. Things sit. You lose deals to whoever called back first, or you win them late and start the relationship apologizing. This means you are good at sales and short of capacity, and capacity is exactly what a rep adds.

**Two. You have good leads coming in, you can keep up with them, and you are not closing.** The conversations happen, the proposals go out, and the answer keeps being no or nothing at all. That means either your own sales skills need work, or you need somebody who already has them. Both are fixable, and both are a sales problem rather than a marketing one.

Notice that both triggers start from the same place, which is that leads are already arriving. That is the condition. If the top of the funnel is dry, neither of these describes you.

## If neither one fits

Then what is missing is the machine that produces leads in the first place, and no salesperson is going to build it for you.

Marketing is the function that owner-operated businesses delay the longest, and I think there are three reasons for it. It is the one most owners understand the least, which makes it hard to evaluate and hard to hire for. The payoff is slower and less legible than a sale, so it always loses the budget argument to something more immediate. And for a long time you did not need it, because the business grew on reputation and referral, which felt like marketing working when really it was the absence of it not yet hurting.

That last one is the trap. Reputation and network are real assets, and they do not scale on demand. They deliver what they deliver. When you decide you want to grow faster than they can carry you, the gap does not announce itself. Things just get quieter than they used to be, and the natural reading of quiet is that you need someone out selling.

A marketing hire builds the thing that makes a sales hire work later. The list, the content, the position in the market, the reason someone thinks of you before they need you, the follow-up that catches people who were not ready the first time. It is unglamorous and it compounds, which is a bad combination for getting funded and a very good one for actually growing.

Most owners I have worked with, once they finally resource it, describe it the same way. They cannot work out how they operated for so long without it.

## The counter-argument, and what is true in it

Someone will tell you that a good sales rep generates their own leads, and that a real hunter does not need marketing handed to them.

There is truth in it. Those people exist. I have met a few and they are genuinely impressive, capable of building a pipeline out of nothing but a phone and a list.

They are also expensive, close to impossible to manage, and generally a poor fit for a business this size. That is not a criticism of them, it is a mismatch of goals. The people who can do that want to go and sign three deals that double your revenue in six months and earn two hundred thousand dollars doing it. If you are built to absorb that, it is a fine problem to have. Most businesses at two million are not built to absorb it, and the version of success where you triple in half a year is more likely to break you than the version where nothing happens at all.

What most owner-operated businesses need is steady, and steady is fed by marketing.

## How to think about the order

The simplest version I can give you is this. Marketing fills the top of the funnel, sales converts what is in it, and you cannot convert what is not there.

If leads are scarce, that is a marketing problem and hiring sales makes it more expensive without making it better. If leads are plentiful and you are drowning in them, that is a capacity problem and a rep is exactly right. If leads are plentiful and you are losing them, that is a skills problem and either training or a rep will solve it.

Work out which of those three you are actually in before you write the job description. It is a ten minute exercise and it is worth more than most of the advice you will get about growth, including this post.
