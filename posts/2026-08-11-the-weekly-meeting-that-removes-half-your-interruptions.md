title: "The weekly meeting that removes half your interruptions"
date: 2026-08-11T09:00:00-07:00
excerpt: "Most of the questions that reach an owner during the day could have waited a week. They arrive daily because there is no scheduled time when they would get answered, and that is a fixable problem that costs one recurring calendar entry."
category: "Owner Blind Spots"
---
For most of the years I ran my business, my door was open and I was proud of it. Anyone could come to me with anything at any time, and I thought that was what a good owner looked like. What it actually looked like, if you had followed me around for a week with a stopwatch, was a man who arrived at seven with three things he intended to do and left at seven having done none of them.

The interruptions were never dramatic. Somebody at the door with a quick question. A message that needed a one line answer. A phone call from a tech who wanted a second opinion before he committed to something. Each one really was small, and each one really did take about five minutes. What none of us count is the ten minutes on the other side, getting back to where we were before, which is the part that actually does the damage. Eight of those in a day is not forty minutes. It is closer to two hours, and it is two hours taken out of the middle of everything else, which is why the day feels shredded rather than merely full.

Here is the part I find harder to say out loud. I liked it. Being the person everyone came to felt like proof the place needed me, and there is a real satisfaction in being able to unstick anything in five minutes flat. That feeling is the trap. I have written before about how often [the owner turns out to be the bottleneck](/insights/the-biggest-bottleneck-is-probably-you/) they have been hunting for, and the constant interruption is the most flattering possible disguise for it. It does not feel like a problem. It feels like being needed.

What finally changed my thinking was noticing what the questions actually were. Almost none of them were urgent. A supplier decision that could have waited until Friday. A question about how to handle a customer who was not going to call back for a week. Something a tech could have worked out himself with twenty minutes of thought. They were not arriving on my desk that morning because they were time critical. They were arriving because there was no other time they were going to be dealt with, and the only mechanism anyone had for getting an answer out of me was to physically stand in front of me until I gave one.

That is the whole insight, and it took me far too long to get to it. The interruptions are a symptom of a missing appointment. If there is no scheduled time when a non-urgent question gets answered, then every non-urgent question becomes an urgent one, because standing at your door is the only channel that works.

The fix is a standing weekly one to one with each of your direct reports. I can hear the groan from here, because everybody knows they should be doing these and a lot of people already are, in a fashion. The ones that work are built differently from the ones that quietly die after six weeks, and the differences are small enough that they are easy to miss.

Thirty minutes, and no longer. This has to save time to be worth anything, and an hour of unstructured conversation once a week is not a saving. Thirty minutes forces both of you to bring what matters. If something genuinely needs an hour, it needs its own meeting with its own preparation, which is a different thing entirely and should be booked as one.

They bring the agenda, not you. This is the rule people break first and it is the one that decides whether the meeting survives. The point of the session is to give your team a reliable place to put things, which means it has to belong to them. What they closed, where they are stuck, what needs a decision from you. If you have items of your own and there is time left, fine, put them at the end. The moment it becomes your status update in disguise, they stop bringing things, and once they stop bringing things they go back to the door.

One more thing on the agenda point, and I would treat this as absolute. Never use these sessions for negative feedback. If you do it once, every future session carries the possibility that this is the week they get told off, and nobody brings you their genuine problems in a meeting they are braced for. Corrective conversations happen separately and immediately, not saved up for Thursday.

You cannot cancel. Ever. This is the load bearing rule and it is the one owners break with the clearest conscience, because something big comes up and it is only a half hour with one person and you will catch them next week. What the team learns from that is that the slot is not real, and a slot that is not real cannot hold anything. They go straight back to the drive by, because the drive by has never once failed them. Move it if you have to. Do not cancel it.

Once the meetings are running, you get one line and you use it every single time. Put it on the list for your one to one. Somebody appears at your door, and unless the building is on fire, that is your answer. If they tell you it cannot wait, listen to the question, because occasionally they are right and you need to know that. Most of the time it can wait, and you say so, and it goes on the list.

The effect I did not expect is the one that matters most. Roughly half of what goes on the list gets solved before the meeting ever happens. Given four days and no way to hand the problem to you, people work it out. They ask a colleague, they look it up, they try the obvious thing and it turns out to be right. By Thursday the item has been crossed off and what you get instead are the things that genuinely needed you. Your team learns more from the ones they solve themselves than they ever would have from your five minute answer, which is the outcome you actually wanted the whole time.

Somebody is doing the arithmetic at this point and objecting that six direct reports means three hours a week of new meetings, which is not obviously a saving. I would say two things to that. The first is that you are underestimating the current cost by a wide margin, because interruptions do not show up anywhere in your calendar and the two hours a day they are taking is invisible to you. The second is that consolidating scattered time into a scheduled block is worth something on its own even if the total were identical, because the rest of your week becomes usable again. Three hours you chose beats two hours a day you did not.

There is a limit to what this fixes, and it is worth naming. If the same question comes back from the same person week after week, you do not have a scheduling problem. You have a question of who owns that decision, and no meeting cadence will solve it. That is [handing over the authority rather than the task](/insights/delegating-authority/), which is a harder piece of work and the one most of us skip. The one to one will show you exactly which decisions those are, though, which is useful in itself. Watch what keeps reappearing on the list.

If you want to try it, the whole thing costs you one recurring calendar entry per person and a conversation explaining what the meeting is for. Give it a month before you judge it. The first two weeks feel like more meetings and no benefit, because your team does not believe the slot is real yet and is still testing whether the door still works. Somewhere around week three the drive bys fall off a cliff, and you get an afternoon back that you had forgotten was available.

Working out which of those questions should have been yours in the first place is harder to see from inside your own business, and it is a fair part of what an [outside perspective](/how-i-work.html) is for.
