Delegation was one of the harder things I had to learn as an owner, and I learned it slowly. For the first stretch I was bad at it and I knew I was bad at it. Then I reached the genuinely dangerous stage, where I was still bad at it but had started to believe I was good. It took me years to work out what was actually going on underneath, and what follows is the version I wish somebody had drawn out for me at the beginning.

There are four rungs to this, and most owners are standing on one of the first two wondering why the whole thing still feels so heavy.

The first rung is handing someone a task and nothing else. No training, no clear picture of what finished looks like, no date. They will usually attempt it, because most people want to do well by you. What comes back will not be what you needed, because you never actually said what you needed. The first time something unexpected happens they will be at your door with a question, and about half the time you will take it back and do it yourself. That feels faster in the moment, and it teaches everyone involved precisely the wrong lesson.

The second rung is where most owners get to and stop. You give them the training, you are clear about the expected result, and you set a deadline. This is a real improvement and the work usually gets done, often close to right. What you will notice is that you are still being interrupted constantly, because the moment anything goes off script they come back to you. You have given them the destination and the map, and you have quietly kept the authority to make any decision the map does not cover. Every unexpected turn is therefore still yours.

That is the part most people miss. Handing over the work and handing over the decisions are two separate acts, and it is the second one that gives you your time back.

The third rung is adding the authority. Training, a clear expectation of the end result, and the explicit right to make whatever calls are necessary to get there. What changes is less the quality of the work than the volume of traffic coming back at you. They stop asking, because they no longer need to ask.

This is the rung that frightens people, and the fear is reasonable. They will make calls you would not have made. Some will be worse than yours. A few will be wrong. What you are buying with that discomfort is a person who can operate without you, and there is no version of this where you get one without paying for it. The alternative is remaining the single point of decision in your own company, which works perfectly well right up until it does not.

The fourth rung is the one worth reaching, and very few people do.

Tell them why it needs to be done. Involve them in defining what a good result looks like and how the process ought to run. Then give them authority over it.

What happens next is different in kind rather than in degree. They do not simply execute your idea competently. They come back with a better way of doing it than the one you had, because they are closer to the work than you are and because you have finally given them permission to think about it instead of just carrying it out.

I watched this happen repeatedly and it surprised me every time. The person doing a job every day knows things about that job that the owner cannot know. The first three rungs all quietly treat that knowledge as irrelevant. Only the fourth one asks for it.

There is a version of delegation that is really just distribution, where you cut your own workload into pieces and hand the pieces out. It reduces your typing. It does not reduce the number of decisions routed through you, and decisions, not tasks, are what actually caps how large a business can get. Most owners who feel like the bottleneck have distributed a great deal and delegated almost nothing.

Telling those two apart from inside your own company is genuinely difficult, and it is one of the places where [an outside perspective](/how-i-work.html) tends to earn its keep. It is hard to see that you are the constraint when you are also the one holding the measuring tape.

If you tell people what to do in the right way, they will do what you need. They will only do great things if you make them part of the story.
